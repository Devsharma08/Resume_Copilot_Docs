# Signal or Noise? — Consolidated Research README

> **Purpose:** One working document consolidating the user's notes, findings reported in the paper, critical observations inferred from those findings, and their relevance to the proposed GADS/EGCA resume-screening architecture.
>
> **Status:** Research notes and project-planning document — not a reproduction of the paper and not a claim that the proposed architecture has been experimentally validated.

## 1. Paper details

- **Title:** *Signal or Noise? Evaluating Large Language Models in Resume Screening Across Contextual Variations and Human Expert Benchmarks*
- **Authors:** Aryan Varshney and Venkat Ram Reddy Ganuthula
- **Paper:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.08019
- **Models studied:** Claude, GPT, and Gemini
- **Domain:** Resume screening against job descriptions
- **Central question:** Do large language models (LLMs) make stable, meaningful screening judgments, or do their scores change substantially with contextual prompts? How do their judgments compare with human recruitment experts?

## 2. Abstract — consolidated summary

This study investigates whether large language models exhibit consistent behavior (“signal”) or random variation (“noise”) when screening resumes against job descriptions, and how their evaluations compare with human experts. The researchers tested Claude, GPT, and Gemini under four prompt contexts: No Company, Firm1 (MNC), Firm2 (startup), and Reduced Context. They used identical-resume and randomized-resume experiments and compared model outputs with three human recruitment experts.

The reported analyses found significant differences among models in some conditions and significant differences between LLM and human evaluations. GPT showed strong adaptation to company context, Gemini showed selective adaptation, and Claude showed comparatively limited adaptation in the identical-resume experiment. The paper also examined the scoring weights that models and humans described using. Its overall implication is that LLMs can show interpretable patterns and context sensitivity, but their scores should not be assumed to align with human judgments or to be reliable enough for unsupervised high-stakes hiring decisions.

**Important distinction:** Statistical differences in scores are not, by themselves, proof of better or worse hiring accuracy, job-performance prediction, fairness, or candidate quality.

## 3. Research background and motivation

### 3.1 Why resume screening is difficult

Resume screening involves both explicit and less easily measured information:

- **Explicit qualifications:** education, employment history, skills, certifications, internships, projects, and years of experience.
- **Contextual qualifications:** the relevance of experience to a particular role, industry knowledge, adaptability, problem-solving, leadership, and communication.
- **Organizational context:** company size, industry, work structure, expectations, tools, and role responsibilities.

Recruiters may weigh these factors differently. A large organization may use formal qualifications and standardized processes, while a startup may emphasize versatility and the ability to work in changing conditions. These are possible tendencies, not universal rules, and should be translated into explicit job-related criteria rather than stereotypes about company types.

### 3.2 Main concerns motivating the study

1. LLMs may produce different scores for similar inputs or change their judgments when prompts are modified.
2. Different models may interpret the same resume and job description differently.
3. Model scores may diverge from human expert evaluations.
4. Context sensitivity may be useful when it reflects real role requirements, but harmful when irrelevant contextual cues shift scores.
5. LLMs can reproduce or amplify existing societal biases, including potential gender- and race-related disparities.
6. A coherent explanation generated by a model does not necessarily demonstrate that the explanation faithfully represents the process behind its score.
7. Hiring decisions affect people's livelihoods, so reliability, explainability, fairness, and accountability matter.
8. A single prompt or a single run may be insufficient to assess model behavior. Multi-prompt, paraphrase, and repeated-run evaluations can reveal sensitivity that one evaluation misses.

## 4. Research objectives

The study's objectives can be summarized as follows:

1. Determine whether LLM scores differ across contextual conditions.
2. Assess whether company context systematically changes model scores.
3. Explore how LLMs describe the relative importance of resume components and compare those descriptions with human scoring approaches.
4. Measure the magnitude and statistical significance of differences between LLM and human evaluations.
5. Examine whether the observed changes look like meaningful adaptation, inconsistent behavior, or a mixture of both.

## 5. Theoretical framing

### Signal Detection Theory

Signal Detection Theory provides a useful conceptual lens for distinguishing meaningful information from noise in decision-making. In this application, the important question is whether score changes reflect real differences in candidate-role fit or sensitivity to prompt wording and contextual details.

However, score variation alone does not fully operationalize Signal Detection Theory. A rigorous implementation would need a defined target outcome, such as whether a candidate meets a requirement or later demonstrates job-related performance, as well as appropriate measures of false positives, false negatives, sensitivity, and specificity.

### Metacognition and stated reasoning

The paper examines how evaluators describe their own scoring criteria and how those criteria appear to change across contexts. Metacognition generally concerns awareness and regulation of one's own thinking. In LLM evaluation, a model's stated weights or explanation should be treated as a **reported rationale**, not automatic proof of the internal computation that produced the score.

## 6. Experimental methodology

### 6.1 Evaluators

- Three LLMs: Claude, GPT, and Gemini.
- Three human recruitment experts with different recruitment backgrounds.
- The human experts regularly evaluated resumes for Product Manager roles.
- The paper describes different experience profiles, including multinational-company and startup recruitment experience.

The sample of three human experts is small, so it cannot represent the full diversity of professional recruiters.

### 6.2 Dataset

The paper reports using 30 anonymized resumes for Product Manager positions. The resumes varied in education, work experience, technical skills, and career paths.

The experiments included:
- **Identical-resume experiment:** repeated evaluation of a single resume to investigate differences across models and contexts.
- **Randomized-resume experiment:** evaluation of a selection of different resumes to observe behavior across candidates.

The job description concerned an Associate Product Manager role in Gurgaon, Haryana, India. A reduced job-description version was also created by removing detailed information.

### 6.3 Prompt/context conditions

| Condition | Description | Main question |
|---|---|---|
| No Company | Job description without company profile | What happens with role information alone? |
| Firm1 (MNC) | Job description plus multinational-company context | Does an MNC profile change scores or criteria? |
| Firm2 (Startup) | Job description plus startup context | Does startup context change scores or criteria? |
| Reduced Context | Minimal job-description information | What happens when role context is incomplete? |

The MNC profile emphasized more structured processes, hierarchy, training, and formal organizational expectations. The startup profile emphasized a flatter hierarchy, flexible responsibilities, hands-on problem-solving, self-learning, and openness to open-source technologies.

### 6.4 Evaluation procedure

- LLMs were prompted to act as talent-acquisition professionals and score resumes out of 100.
- Models were asked to provide numerical scores and explain how they weighted resume components.
- Human experts evaluated resumes using their own scoring approaches.
- Researchers compared scores across models, contexts, resume sets, and human evaluators.

The LLM and human procedures were not identical: standardized model prompts were compared with human evaluators who could develop their own scoring approaches. This matters when interpreting the differences.

### 6.5 Statistical methods reported

- **ANOVA:** tests whether mean scores differ across groups.
- **Paired t-tests:** test score changes between related conditions, such as No Company versus a company-context condition.
- **Cohen's d:** estimates the magnitude of a difference.
- **Correlation analysis:** explores relationships between evaluators' scores.
- **False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction:** considered to address multiple-testing concerns.
- The paper reports using significance thresholds including `p < 0.10` and `p < 0.01` in its analysis framework.

A p-value does not measure practical importance, correctness, or fairness. Effect sizes, confidence intervals, test assumptions, and multiple-comparison handling should also be examined.

## 7. Main findings reported in the paper

> The figures below reproduce the reported values captured in these research notes. Before quoting them in a formal publication, verify each value against the corresponding original table and its statistical test.

### 7.1 Model scores can differ for the same resume

For the identical-resume experiment in the No Company condition, the reported mean scores were:

- Claude: **69.4**
- GPT: **50.5**
- Gemini: **72.8**
- Reported significance: `p = 0.004`

This indicates a significant difference among the models in that condition. A common resume and job description did not lead to a common scoring scale.

For the identical-resume MNC condition, reported scores were:

- Claude: **66.5**
- GPT: **76.1**
- Gemini: **76.7**

The models' relative scores changed substantially compared with No Company.

### 7.2 GPT showed strong context sensitivity

In the identical-resume experiment, GPT's mean score reportedly increased from **50.5** in No Company to **76.1** for Firm1 and **77.8** for Firm2. The reported paired comparisons were significant at `p < 0.001`, with reported Cohen's d values of approximately `-1.92` and `-2.02` under the paper's comparison convention.

Interpretation: GPT's scores were highly responsive to the added company context. This is evidence of sensitivity to context, not proof that the resulting scores were more accurate or that GPT truly understood the company.

### 7.3 Gemini showed selective context sensitivity

In the identical-resume experiment, Gemini reportedly increased from **72.8** in No Company to **76.7** for Firm1, with `p = 0.038`. Its reported Firm2 score was **73.6**, with `p = 0.498` for the comparison with baseline.

Interpretation: Gemini did not react equally to every company context. Its adaptation appeared context-dependent.

### 7.4 Claude showed comparatively limited adaptation in the identical-resume experiment

Claude reportedly scored **69.4** in No Company, **66.5** for Firm1, and **73.3** for Firm2. The reported contextual comparisons were not statistically significant in that experiment.

Interpretation: Claude appeared more stable under those tested conditions. Stability can be useful, but it does not necessarily mean that its scores are accurate or appropriately sensitive to genuine job requirements.

### 7.5 Model differences depended on the resume set

In the randomized-resume No Company condition, reported mean scores were:

- Claude: **66.6**
- GPT: **64.3**
- Gemini: **65.4**
- Reported significance: `p = 0.892`

In the identical-resume No Company condition, the difference among models was significant (`p = 0.004`).

For randomized resumes in Firm1, reported means were Claude **67.5**, GPT **58.5**, and Gemini **73.5**, with reported significance `p = 0.018`.

Interpretation: apparent agreement among model averages depends on the experiment and the resume set. Similar group means do not prove that the models rank each individual candidate similarly.

### 7.6 LLM evaluations differed significantly from human evaluations

The paper reports significant differences between evaluator groups in the randomized-resume conditions:

| Condition | Reported p-value |
|---|---:|
| No Company | `0.0015` |
| Firm1 | `0.0007` |
| Firm2 | `0.0065` |
| Reduced Context | `0.0002` |

The paper describes score gaps between LLMs and human evaluators, including gaps of approximately 20–35 points in Reduced Context and individual gaps reaching 40 points.

Interpretation: internal consistency and agreement with human judgment are separate properties. An LLM can be stable and still differ from human evaluators. Conversely, disagreement with a human does not by itself prove that the LLM is wrong, because human evaluators can also be inconsistent or biased.

### 7.7 Reduced Context raised reliability concerns

In the randomized-resume Reduced Context condition, the reported scores were:

- Claude: **65.6**
- GPT: **82.9**
- Gemini: **72.0**
- Reported significance among models: `p = 0.002`

The notes report GPT's score rising from **64.3** in randomized No Company to **82.9** in Reduced Context, described in the paper as approximately **28.9% score inflation**. The reported human–AI group comparison for Reduced Context was `F(5, 54) = 18.23`, `p = 0.0002`.

Interpretation: reduced information can lead to unexpectedly high or divergent scores. The direction and magnitude of the effect should be verified in the original analysis before treating it as a general property of GPT or of LLMs.

### 7.8 Human experts also differed

The paper describes different evaluator approaches:
- One expert generally used relatively stable scoring across contexts.
- Another adapted criteria more noticeably to startup versus MNC context.
- A third expert reportedly assigned zero scores in some later conditions, creating interpretive and data-quality concerns.

Interpretation: human judgment should not be treated as a flawless or universal ground truth. A stronger benchmark would use multiple independent experts, a clear rubric, documented adjudication, and measures of inter-rater agreement.

|---|---:|
| Relevant work experience | 30% |
| Education | 20% |
| Internships | 20% |
| Skills and certifications | 15% |
| Achievements | 10% |
| Positions of responsibility | 5% |

The notes capture the following Firm1 (MNC) weights:

| Component | Reported weight |
|---|---:|
| Work experience | 25% |
| Education | 20% |
| Internships | 15% |
| Skills and certifications | 15% |
| Achievements and leadership | 15% |
| Soft skills and extracurricular activities | 10% |

The reported schemes show a change in stated criteria after context was added.

### 8.2 Claude's reported criteria

The paper's reported discussion describes Claude as comparatively stable, with emphasis on:
- Technical skills and relevant experience: approximately 30–35%.
- Education: approximately 20%.
- Product thinking and analysis: approximately 15–25%.
- Soft skills and leadership: approximately 10–15%.
- Cultural fit: approximately 10–15%.

These ranges should not be added together as if they were a single exact weighting scheme; they summarize reported ranges across contexts.

### 8.3 Human scoring approaches

The paper describes human evaluators drawing on their experience and using different weighting schemes. One reported example for Expert 2 allocated:
- **Firm1:** 25% industry experience, 25% tools familiarity, 50% relevant experience.
- **Firm2:** 75% experience, 25% industry fit.

Interpretation: the study suggests that evaluators describe different criteria and contextual adaptations. However, model-generated explanations are not guaranteed to be faithful accounts of internal model computations. A rigorous follow-up should test whether stated weights predict how scores actually change when individual resume components are controlled.

## 9. Problems and risks highlighted by the paper

1. **Prompt sensitivity:** wording and context may change a score.
2. **Model disagreement:** different models may score the same candidate differently.
3. **LLM–human disagreement:** AI scores may not match human expert evaluations.
4. **Context overreaction:** adding company information can shift scores even when the resume stays constant.
5. **Incomplete information:** reduced job context can lead to divergent or inflated scores.
6. **Fairness concerns:** models may reflect or amplify societal biases.
7. **Unverified reasoning:** a model's explanation may not faithfully describe the mechanism behind its output.
8. **Human benchmark limitations:** recruiter scores also vary and can contain bias.
9. **Limited sample size:** three human experts and a narrow role domain limit generalizability.
10. **Statistical interpretation:** significance does not establish correctness, predictive validity, or practical value.
11. **Changing models:** newer model versions may behave differently from the versions studied.
12. **High-stakes consequences:** hiring requires stronger safeguards than low-impact use cases.

## 10. Critical observations inferred from the findings

The following are analytical conclusions drawn from the paper, not necessarily direct claims made by the authors.

### 10.1 A score is not a validated measure of suitability

A score of 85/100 should not be interpreted as an 85% probability of success or as proof of candidate quality. The scale requires calibration and validation against a meaningful target. Without this, scores are best treated as comparative signals with uncertainty.

### 10.2 Consistency, agreement, validity, and fairness are different

These properties must be tested separately:

- **Repeatability:** Does the same system give similar outputs when the same input is evaluated repeatedly?
- **Cross-model agreement:** Do different models reach similar conclusions?
- **Human agreement:** Do model decisions align with trained human evaluators?
- **Validity:** Do decisions measure job-relevant qualifications or predict an appropriate outcome?
- **Fairness:** Are comparable candidates treated comparably, and do outcomes show unjustified group disparities?
- **Calibration:** Do score values correspond to empirically meaningful levels of suitability or probability?

A system can perform well on one dimension and poorly on another.

### 10.3 Context sensitivity is not automatically a benefit

Context should influence evaluation only when it changes genuine role requirements. A startup label or MNC label should not itself be a reason to prefer or penalize a candidate. The job-relevant criteria that follow from organizational context should be made explicit and tested.

### 10.4 Missing evidence is not negative evidence

If a candidate does not provide a public GitHub profile, a public portfolio, or a particular certificate, the system cannot automatically conclude that the candidate lacks the skill. The correct state may be “not observed” or “not verified,” depending on the claim and the available evidence.

### 10.5 Model explanations require empirical checks

If a model says it assigns 30% weight to work experience, controlled tests can vary work experience while keeping other resume features constant. If scores do not respond in a way consistent with the stated weight, the explanation should not be treated as a faithful computational account.

### 10.6 Human experts are useful benchmarks, not infallible truth

Multiple independent evaluators, structured rubrics, inter-rater reliability measures, and adjudication can make human comparisons stronger. Human disagreement should be measured and reported rather than hidden.

### 10.7 Statistical significance is not practical correctness

A low p-value indicates evidence against a statistical null hypothesis under the assumptions of the test. It does not prove that one model is better, fairer, more accurate, or more useful for hiring.

### 10.8 Resume screening is not the same as job-performance prediction

This study mainly examines screening scores and their variation. Demonstrating that one screening method predicts future job performance would require suitable outcome data, careful study design, and controls for confounding factors.

## 11. Proposed implications for automated screening

Based on the paper and the critical observations above:

1. Use explicit job requirements and a documented scoring rubric.
2. Separate mandatory eligibility requirements from preference-based ranking.
3. Record the evidence behind every extracted claim.
4. Treat uncertain or missing information explicitly rather than silently guessing.
5. Test repeated-run and paraphrase sensitivity.
6. Compare rankings as well as raw scores.
7. Evaluate against multiple human experts and report inter-rater agreement.
8. Audit outcomes for demographic and other unjustified disparities.
9. Route ambiguous, conflicting, or high-impact cases to human review.
10. Maintain an audit trail explaining which evidence and policy rules affected each outcome.
11. Avoid treating model-generated explanations as verified internal reasoning.
12. Validate the system before making claims about screening accuracy or fairness.

## 12. Connection to the user's GADS / EGCA proposal

### 12.1 Project framing

The user's proposal is to develop **EGCA (Evidence-Governed Candidate Assessment)** as a focused B.Tech final-year/minor project and possible whitepaper. **GADS (Governed AI Decision Systems)** is the broader governance concept. The goal is not to claim that an autonomous hiring system has been solved, but to design and evaluate a more auditable candidate-claim verification layer.

### 12.2 Core design principle

Separate four questions:

1. **What does the resume claim?**
2. **What evidence supports the claim?**
3. **Does the candidate satisfy mandatory, explicit policy requirements?**
4. **How should eligible candidates be prioritized, and which cases need human review?**

An LLM can help extract and normalize information, but it should not be the sole authority for verifying claims or applying hard eligibility rules.

### 12.3 Proposed two-stage architecture

#### Stage 1 — Initial screening and candidate-pool discovery

- Parse resumes into structured claims about skills, education, experience, projects, and credentials.
- Normalize different terms into a canonical skill taxonomy.
- Match claims against a role blueprint using explicit criteria and lightweight semantic matching.
- Optionally use a skill/prerequisite graph to represent relationships between technologies and competencies.
- Create a preliminary ranked pool.
- Use an **Adaptive Gate** to determine how many candidates should proceed to deeper verification.

The preliminary stage is for discovery and efficient triage, not final verification.

#### Adaptive Gate / buffered pool

- Let `K` be the desired number of candidates in the final shortlist.
- Let `K′` be a larger buffered candidate pool forwarded to Stage 2.
- A simple design is `K′ = K × buffer_multiplier`, with the multiplier selected by a documented rule.
- Gate depth can be informed by applicant volume, role complexity, score distribution, and uncertainty.
- If too few candidates pass verification, progressively expand the pool without relaxing mandatory criteria.

The gate should not silently discard uncertain candidates or let popularity, resume polish, or unsupported model confidence replace job-related requirements.

#### Stage 2 — Evidence verification and governance

- Verify extracted claims using available, relevant, authorized evidence.
- Record source, identity linkage, relevance, freshness, and limitations.
- Classify evidence strength.
- Use a bounded skill graph for carefully constrained inferences between related skills.
- Apply mandatory requirements using a deterministic policy engine such as OPA/Rego.
- Calculate an uncertainty-aware assessment score only after keeping hard policy decisions separate.
- Route uncertain, conflicting, or insufficiently supported cases to human review.
- Maintain an audit trail for evidence, rules, scores, and review outcomes.

### 12.4 Evidence states

The proposed evidence ledger uses these states:

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `NOT_OBSERVED` | No relevant evidence was observed; this is not proof the claim is false. |
| `SELF_CLAIMED` | The claim appears in the candidate's resume or self-report but has not been independently verified. |
| `INFERRED` | The claim is inferred from related evidence; it is not direct evidence. |
| `ARTIFACT_BACKED` | A relevant artifact supports the claim, subject to provenance and authenticity checks. |
| `ASSESSED` | A suitable assessment provides evidence of the claimed competency. |

Evidence status is not the same as truth. Even an artifact-backed claim can require validation, and a missing public artifact should not automatically count against a candidate.

### 12.5 Candidate verification and external evidence

External enrichment from portfolios, GitHub, LinkedIn, or other sources should be designed with safeguards:

- Prefer candidate-provided or explicitly authorized sources.
- Link the source to the correct candidate and claim.
- Check relevance, provenance, freshness, and authenticity where feasible.
- Do not infer competence merely from profile presence, follower count, activity volume, or polished presentation.
- Treat private or unavailable sources as unobservable, not negative evidence.
- Provide alternative evidence routes, such as uploaded work samples or structured technical assessments.
- Avoid invasive collection of personal or irrelevant information.
- Document how conflicting sources are resolved and allow correction of inaccurate records.

### 12.6 Deterministic policy adjudication

Mandatory rules should be isolated from the LLM. For example, if a role requires a legally or operationally necessary qualification, the rule should be explicit and auditable.

- `P_hard` represents deterministic hard-policy adjudication.
- The policy engine should not allow a high semantic match or a large number of optional achievements to override a failed mandatory condition.
- Missing information may require review rather than an automatic negative decision.
- Policies should be job-related, documented, and reviewed for legality and fairness.

### 12.7 Uncertainty-aware assessment

The proposed GADS concept includes a governed closeness metric, `M_G`, that combines multiple signals such as:

- `E_prov`: evidence provenance/strength.
- `S_sem`: semantic alignment with role requirements.
- `U_unc`: uncertainty penalty.
- `P_hard`: deterministic hard-policy outcome.

A conceptual form is:

`M_G = f(E_prov, S_sem, U_unc)`, subject to the hard gate `P_hard`.

This is a design concept, not yet a validated formula. Exact normalization, weights, thresholds, and the treatment of missing evidence must be specified and evaluated empirically. Hard policy adjudication should remain independent of the LLM's semantic score.

The proposed outcome states are:

- `QUALIFIED`
- `NOT_QUALIFIED`
- `HUMAN_REVIEW`

The third state matters: the system should be able to abstain when evidence is inadequate or conflicting instead of forcing a false sense of certainty.

### 12.8 Policy-bounded priority queue

The proposed Policy-Bounded Priority Queue (PBPQ) maintains a shortlist of eligible candidates under explicit constraints.

- Rank only after applying relevant mandatory requirements.
- Preserve the evidence and uncertainty behind each ranking.
- Use a documented, role-specific benchmark rather than an opaque or self-reinforcing “ideal candidate.”
- Prevent optional achievements from overriding hard failures.
- Define stopping and pool-expansion rules in advance.
- Evaluate the ranking under perturbations, alternative prompts, and different candidate sets.

### 12.9 Proposed audit and data entities

The design notes include:

- **EvidenceLedger:** candidate claims, sources, evidence states, provenance, and verification history.
- **ComplianceCertificate:** a structured record of which policy checks were evaluated and their outcomes.
- **AuditTrailLog:** append-only record of decisions, score changes, rule evaluations, and human interventions.

These records should support traceability without retaining unnecessary personal information. Access control, retention periods, data minimization, and correction mechanisms are part of the design.

The paper does not test EGCA. It motivates several hypotheses that EGCA could test.

|---|---|---|
| Models score the same resume differently | Explicit rubric and controlled scoring pipeline | Repeated-run score variation |

## 14. Proposed experimental plan for EGCA

A focused minor project should be small enough to implement and evaluate credibly.

### Baselines

Compare EGCA with a few clear baselines, such as:

1. Keyword/rule-based matching.
2. Embedding-based semantic matching.
3. LLM-only scoring.
4. EGCA with provenance tracking, hard-policy separation, and uncertainty-aware review.

### Test scenarios

- Same resume evaluated repeatedly.
- Equivalent prompts with paraphrased wording.
- Relevant MNC/startup context changes.
- Irrelevant company-label changes.
- Reduced or incomplete job descriptions.
- Claims supported by direct artifacts versus self-report only.
- Missing or unavailable external evidence.
- Conflicting evidence.
- Mandatory requirement present, absent, or uncertain.
- Candidate resumes with equivalent qualifications but different writing styles.

### Metrics

- Score and ranking stability.
- Pairwise ranking agreement.
- Precision/recall for clearly defined eligibility rules.
- False-positive and false-negative rates for mandatory checks.
- Evidence coverage and provenance completeness.
- Human-review rate and resolution outcomes.
- Calibration, if scores are intended to represent probabilities.
- Fairness metrics where lawful, ethically collected, and statistically supportable.
- Runtime and cost for practical feasibility.

### Evaluation cautions

- Do not claim fairness merely because scores are stable.
- Do not claim hiring accuracy without a defensible target label.
- Do not treat a small synthetic dataset as proof of real-world effectiveness.
- Avoid collecting sensitive demographic information unless the research has a legitimate, lawful, ethical evaluation plan.
- Report failure cases, limitations, and uncertainty rather than only aggregate performance.

## 15. Research contribution and whitepaper framing

A defensible contribution is not “AI can hire better than humans.” A stronger framing is:

> **EGCA is a prototype architecture for evidence-governed candidate assessment that separates resume-claim extraction, evidence provenance, deterministic eligibility rules, uncertainty-aware ranking, and human review. The project investigates whether these separations improve auditability and robustness compared with simpler screening baselines.**

Potential contributions:

1. A claim-level evidence ledger with explicit provenance states.
2. Bounded skill inference with a clear distinction between direct and inferred evidence.
3. Deterministic hard-policy adjudication separated from semantic scoring.
4. Uncertainty-based abstention and human routing.
5. A buffered candidate pool with progressive expansion rather than relaxation of mandatory requirements.
6. A reproducible evaluation suite for context sensitivity, missing evidence, and ranking stability.

These are proposed contributions. Their novelty and effectiveness need to be assessed through a literature review and experiments before being asserted in a paper.

## 16. Limitations and open questions for EGCA

1. How should `E_prov`, `S_sem`, and `U_unc` be normalized and combined?
2. How can uncertainty be calibrated rather than guessed?
3. What qualifies as sufficiently strong evidence for different types of claims?
4. How should skill-graph inference avoid unjustified conclusions?
5. How can the system avoid disadvantaging candidates who have less public work or fewer opportunities to produce artifacts?
6. How will a candidate dispute or correct an incorrect claim extraction?
7. What evidence can be legally and ethically collected in the target jurisdiction?
8. How will policy versions, prompt versions, and model versions be logged?
9. How can ranking performance be evaluated without leaking protected or irrelevant information?
10. What level of human review is practical, and how will reviewer disagreement be handled?
11. What evidence would be required before the system could be used in a real hiring process?

## 17. Practical recommendations

1. Start with a small, controlled prototype and a narrow role definition.
2. Make job requirements explicit before implementing scoring.
3. Build evidence provenance and the `NOT_OBSERVED` state early.
4. Keep mandatory rules deterministic and independent from the LLM.
5. Make the human-review outcome a first-class result.
6. Log every material change in evidence, policy, score, and decision.
7. Test against baselines and adversarially incomplete inputs.
8. Document limitations and avoid unsupported claims about fairness or accuracy.
9. Treat the system as decision support, not as an autonomous hiring authority.
10. Before real-world deployment, obtain appropriate legal, privacy, security, and domain review.

## 18. Final consolidated conclusion

The paper *Signal or Noise?* reports that LLM resume-screening scores vary across models and contexts, that some models react strongly to added company information, and that model scores differ significantly from human expert evaluations in the reported experiments. Its findings do not establish that human judgment is always correct or that one model is universally better. Instead, they show why score stability, human agreement, validity, calibration, and fairness must be tested separately.

The user's GADS/EGCA proposal responds to these concerns by separating candidate claims from supporting evidence, evidence from mandatory policy checks, and eligibility from ranking. It introduces explicit provenance states, bounded skill inference, deterministic policy adjudication, uncertainty-aware human review, and an auditable shortlist process.

The central research question for EGCA should be empirical:

> **Does an evidence-governed, uncertainty-aware screening pipeline reduce unsupported decisions and improve auditability and ranking robustness compared with keyword-based, embedding-based, and LLM-only baselines—without introducing unjustified disadvantages for candidates with less observable evidence?**

Answering that question with transparent methods, suitable baselines, and honest limitations would provide a stronger foundation for a final-year minor project and potential whitepaper than claiming that the architecture already solves bias or hiring accuracy.
