Why I think this is better

1. It gives GADS a very clear purpose.

Step 1 answers:

“Who appears relevant enough to investigate further?”

GADS answers:

“Among these seemingly relevant candidates, which claims are actually supported, which mandatory requirements are satisfied, and where are we uncertain?”

That separation is conceptually strong and much easier to explain in a research paper.

Your current feasibility document already separates extraction, evidence evaluation, policy adjudication and scoring/routing, but currently treats them as one four-stage pipeline.

2. Your reuse of Step-1 data is a very good engineering decision.

Don't make GADS re-read the entire PDF.

Store:

Candidate
├── structured claims
├── normalized skills
├── skill graph representation
├── preliminary score
└── evidence references

Then GADS consumes those objects.

That makes your second stage genuinely incremental rather than duplicating computation.

3. But there is one serious problem with “Top-K → GADS.”

Suppose:

K = 10

Your preliminary model selects 10 candidates.

Candidate #3 looks excellent semantically but actually fails a mandatory degree requirement.

GADS rejects them.

Now you have only 9 candidates.

But candidate #11 may have been eligible and should have entered.

So do not use the preliminary Top-K as the final candidate pool.

Instead:

Initial Top-K' → GADS → Final Top-K

For example:

1000 candidates
      ↓
Pre-screen
      ↓
Top 50
      ↓
GADS
      ↓
Final Top 10

Or apply cheap mandatory eligibility filters before the preliminary queue, while reserving expensive evidence analysis for the finalists.

This is probably the single most important architectural correction to your idea.

4. I would also change what PBPQ means in the two stages

Don't have one PBPQ that pretends to produce the final ranking and then run GADS afterwards.

Use two concepts:

Preliminary Queue

“Which candidates deserve deeper analysis?”

Governed Queue

“After evidence/policy/uncertainty analysis, which candidates remain strongest?”

That gives you:

PBPQ_pre
     ↓
GADS
     ↓
PBPQ_final

This also creates a very good research experiment:

How much does the governed ranking change compared with the initial similarity-based ranking?

That is much more interesting scientifically than simply showing that GADS produces a score.

5. Your research paper becomes stronger too

You now have a natural baseline vs governed system comparison:

System	Uses evidence	Hard policy	Uncertainty	Human routing
Preliminary screening	No/limited	Basic	No	No
GADS/EGCA	Yes	Yes	Yes	Yes

Then you can test:

Baseline ranking
       vs

under your four existing scenarios:

Your current feasibility plan already defines these experiments.

This gives the paper a much cleaner argument:

“Does adding an evidence-governance layer after conventional candidate screening reduce specific failure modes?”

That's a strong, testable research question.

6. One thing I would NOT do

Don't make Step 1 itself too sophisticated.

If Step 1 already contains:

evidence provenance,
uncertainty,
policy adjudication,
sophisticated M_G,
adversarial defense,

then GADS has nothing meaningful left to contribute.

Step 1 should intentionally be the baseline.

Something like:

Step 1:
Resume → structured claims → skill normalization → semantic fit → preliminary queue

Step 2:
Preliminary candidates → evidence → policy → uncertainty → governed score

That separation gives you a clean experimental story.

7. One research-design warning

There is a danger in only running GADS on the preselected candidates:

GADS can never recover a good candidate that Step 1 completely missed.

So your paper should explicitly say:

GADS governs and refines the candidate representation within the candidate pool admitted by the preliminary screening stage.

Don't claim that GADS improves the entire hiring process unless you evaluate candidates outside that pool too.

A nice solution is to keep a small evaluation set outside the preliminary Top-K and test whether the first-stage filter itself misses relevant candidates.

Yes. At this point, I would make the progressive selection mechanism itself a formal algorithmic component of the research, rather than an implementation detail.

The key idea should be:

Do not decide beforehand that “20% of candidates go to GADS.” Instead, start with a justified initial pool, run GADS, measure whether the required number of sufficiently qualified candidates has been found, and progressively expand the pool when the evidence says the current pool is insufficient.

Your existing project already has the right ingredients for this: preliminary ranking, PBPQ, evidence evaluation, P_hard, M_G, uncertainty and a final shortlist.

1. First define what “enough candidates” means

You need a variable that represents the actual recruitment requirement.

Let:

H = number of candidates ultimately required

Suppose a company wants:

H = 5 hires

You should not stop as soon as GADS finds five people.

You need a candidate reserve because some may later be rejected, withdrawn, or require human review.

So define:

R = required governed candidates

where R > H.

The exact reserve factor should become a configurable project parameter, not a hard-coded universal truth.

For your experiments, you can test different reserve levels rather than claiming one is universally correct.

2. The first stage should establish a baseline

This is where I agree strongly with you:

The system needs a principled answer for “how far down the Stage-1 ranking are we willing to go?”

Don't choose:

Top 10%

just because it sounds reasonable.

Instead, use your synthetic ground-truth data to determine a minimum acceptable Stage-1 coverage threshold.

The key concept is recall.

Suppose, after running your complete GADS process on the experimental dataset, you know which candidates actually satisfy your research-defined target conditions.

Now ask:

“If I only keep the top X% from Stage 1, how many of those relevant candidates do I recover?”

For example:

Stage-1 pool	Relevant candidates recovered
Top 5%	72%
Top 10%	88%
Top 15%	95%
Top 20%	98%

You could then define a research constraint such as:

Choose the smallest Stage-1 pool that achieves at least a predefined coverage target.

That's a much stronger justification than:

“We decided to use top 15%.”

3. Formulate this as an optimization problem

This is where your research becomes considerably cleaner.

You want to minimize expensive GADS computation while maintaining sufficient coverage.

Conceptually:

Minimize:
    Cost of GADS evaluations

Subject to:
    Recall of relevant candidates >= τ

Where:

Cost = number of candidates subjected to expensive Stage 2
τ = required coverage threshold

You can evaluate several τ values in experiments.

So your algorithm is solving:

“How few candidates can we deeply assess without losing too many candidates that should have survived?”

That's a legitimate research question.

5. But there should be a second stopping condition

This is important.

You don't want:

“We have 10 candidates, therefore stop.”

Suppose candidate #10 has:

M_G = 61

and candidate #11 has:

M_G = 60.8

There is essentially no separation.

The current shortlist may be unstable.

So I'd use two stopping conditions:

Condition A — Quantity

Enough candidates have satisfied the Stage-2 requirements.

Qualified_GADS ≥ R
Condition B — Boundary stability

The candidates near the current cutoff are no longer producing meaningful changes when another batch is evaluated.

Conceptually:

|M_boundary_previous - M_boundary_current| < ε

or the final PBPQ remains stable after expansion.

This is particularly useful because your existing research already cares about ranking stability, including arrival-order stability of PBPQ.

6. You also need an “uncertainty trigger”

This is something I would add to the design.

Suppose GADS processes 50 candidates and finds:

40 → clearly acceptable
5  → clearly unsuitable
5  → highly uncertain

Those five uncertain candidates shouldn't simply disappear.

Your existing architecture already says high uncertainty should route candidates to humans.

So the progressive algorithm should understand three outcomes:

GADS result

│
├── Clearly unsuitable
│
└── Uncertain → Human review

Then the stopping decision is based on confirmed candidates, not merely high M_G.

Use the Stage-1 score distribution.

If GADS finds many strong candidates immediately, expand slowly or stop.

8. Protect against Stage-1 mistakes

This is one of the biggest weaknesses of a two-stage system.

Suppose the real good candidate is ranked #173 in Stage 1 because the preliminary model misunderstood their resume.

If you simply evaluate:

Top 50
→ GADS

So I would introduce a small boundary exploration mechanism.

For every expansion round, evaluate:

Primary batch
+
Boundary/uncertainty candidates

For example:

Top 50
      +
Candidates 51–60 with unusual/uncertain Stage-1 signals

This gives GADS a chance to recover candidates that Stage 1 scored incorrectly.

This is especially important given that your project explicitly studies heterogeneous candidates, keyword stuffing, candidates without public profiles, and adjacent technologies.
