Do not treat “resume screening” as one scoring problem. Treat it as four separate trust problems:
What was written → What can be supported → What rules are satisfied → How should qualified candidates be ordered.

2. The refined problem taxonomy

Your earlier one-word answer, “Unverifiability,” is still useful as the root problem, but it is too broad to use as the complete research taxonomy.

I would decompose it into:

Problem	What current screening does	EGCA response
P1 — Untrusted document input	Treats extracted resume content as trustworthy	Trust boundary + controlled extraction
P2 — Claim unverifiability	Treats self-reported claims as facts	Evidence classification
P3 — Vocabulary brittleness	Relies heavily on matching terminology	Skill normalization + semantic matching + graph
P4 — Unsupported inference	Makes implicit equivalences without explaining them	Bounded skill-graph inference
P5 — Mandatory requirement override	High similarity can compensate for missing requirements	Independent P_hard policy gate
P6 — False certainty	Produces a score even when evidence is weak/missing	U_unc + human routing
P7 — Missing-data penalty	Absence of a public profile can look like absence of skill	NOT_OBSERVED ≠ negative evidence
P8 — Opaque decision path	Difficult to reconstruct why candidate was ranked/rejected	Evidence Ledger + AuditTrailLog
P9 — Ranking/selection instability	Candidate order or cutoff can affect shortlist	PEGS + E4 stability test
P10 — Computational cost	Full expensive evaluation of everyone is inefficient	Adaptive Gate + buffered expansion
P11 — Pool insufficiency	Systems may relax criteria when not enough candidates qualify	Explicit pool-exhaustion state
P12 — Bias/fairness	Historical or proxy patterns can affect screening	Explicitly bounded; not claimed solved
P13 — Real-world validity	Synthetic experiments do not prove hiring effectiveness	Explicit research limitation

That decomposition is much stronger for a paper because now every problem has a corresponding architectural control.

3. Stage-by-stage mapping
P0 — Trust Boundary
Purpose

Establish that the resume is input data, not an instruction source.

Components

The original uploaded document is stored as an immutable source artifact.

Then extraction operates on a controlled representation of that document.

Problems addressed

Primarily:

P1 — untrusted document input

P1/P2 — document-level prompt injection and claim contamination

Guardrails

The LLM should only be allowed to produce a fixed schema such as:

CandidateClaim
    ├── claim_type
    ├── technology
    ├── duration
    ├── context
    ├── source_span
    └── confidence

Anything outside that contract is not allowed to become part of the downstream decision state.

Your feasibility report already makes schema validation and malformed-output containment part of Stage 1.

Important refinement

I would not write in the paper that schema validation completely solves white-fonting or prompt injection.

It doesn't.

Schema validation limits what the model can return, but malicious text can still influence the model inside the allowed fields.

So your architecture should describe this as:

Prompt-injection containment, not complete prompt-injection elimination.

For white-fonting specifically, a stronger future refinement would compare the document's visual/text representation against the extracted text layer so suspicious hidden content can be detected.

That distinction will make your research claim much more defensible.

Here is the refactored architecture. It applies the fixes from my earlier review, and I've renamed the IDs to avoid the collisions: failure modes are F1–F13, invariants are I1–I7, and phases go by name.

Principle

Five decisions, each owned by exactly one component, and no component decides two of them:

Decision	Owner	May the LLM decide it?
What was written	Extraction	Yes, discovery only
What is supported	Evidence connectors + ledger	No
What is eligible	Policy engine (OPA)	No
What order	Scorer	No
What needs a human	Router	No
Pipeline
A. INGEST      snapshot → hidden-text check (SUSPECT flag)
               LLM extraction → CandidateClaim{tech, duration, context, source_span}
               grounding check: span must exist in snapshot AND contain the tech, else drop
               claims are capped at SELF_CLAIMED; extraction cannot emit evidence states
               normalize to graph nodes
B. STAGE 1     semantic score → preliminary queue (non-governing; tie-break by candidate_id)
C. GATE        K' = ceil(R / (1 − f)) × margin, f = P_hard failure rate measured on dev split
               adjusted by score-gap analysis, bounded to [K_min, N]
D. STAGE 2     per candidate, in cheap-first order:
   1 evidence connectors → ledger (only source of ARTIFACT_BACKED / ASSESSED)
   2 policy P_hard (tri-valued): PASS / FAIL / INDETERMINATE
        FAIL → INELIGIBLE(reason), stop, no scoring
        INDETERMINATE → NEEDS_REVIEW
   3 graph inference → INFERRED (depth-limited, never satisfies P_hard)
   4 score M_G = w_q·Q + w_e·E_prov     (U_unc is not in the score; G_adapt weight = 0 in core runs)
   5 route: U_unc > τ, SUSPECT, or LLM failure → NEEDS_REVIEW, else QUALIFIED_AUTO
E. EXPANSION   stop only when |QUALIFIED_AUTO| ≥ R AND top-R is stable across the last two batches
               otherwise next fixed batch; pool exhausted → insufficiency report + P_hard failure breakdown
               NEEDS_REVIEW is reported separately and never counts toward R
AUDIT          cross-cutting; an invariant checker replays I1–I7 over the log on every run
What changed and why
Evidence is no longer produced by extraction. Only connectors can assign ARTIFACT_BACKED or ASSESSED. Injected or hallucinated text is therefore capped at SELF_CLAIMED by construction, which is stronger than schema validation. It still contaminates the Stage-1 rank and takes K′ slots, and that cost should be stated openly.
P_hard became three-valued. A missing mandatory item is INDETERMINATE, not FAIL. This is what keeps NOT_OBSERVED ≠ negative consistent with the hard gate. The default I propose is that SELF_CLAIMED is enough to PASS at screening, since verification is a later hiring stage. NOT_OBSERVED gives INDETERMINATE, and INFERRED never passes. Each requirement can override this default. This is the one design decision you must make yourself.
U_unc moved out of the score. It now drives routing only, which removes the indirect penalty that violated E2.
E_prov separates stuffed from honest candidates by claim specificity, not by artifact presence. Specificity means context, duration, and a grounded span. The artifact contribution is capped, so lacking a public profile costs a bounded amount, and E2's ≤5-point bound is then enforced by design.
Policy runs before scoring. A FAIL exits early, so failed candidates cost no graph or scoring work.
Expansion has two stopping conditions and a review-aware count. A shortlist cannot reach R through candidates no one has cleared.
The buffer is derived, not guessed. K′ comes from the measured failure rate.
The system is deterministic end to end. With a total order and a cached extraction, E4 becomes a bug detector. For a real stability test, use incremental arrival or repeated extraction runs.
Invariants, all machine-checkable from the audit log
I1: an SELF_CLAIMED claim never appears as ARTIFACT_BACKED without a connector record.
I2: NOT_OBSERVED never lowers M_G.
I3: P_hard FAIL means the candidate is never QUALIFIED_AUTO.
I4: every INFERRED item carries its graph path and depth.
I5: U_unc above τ means NEEDS_REVIEW.
I6: pool exhausted means an insufficiency report, and the policy version is unchanged during the run.
I7: every state transition has a provenance record.
Failure mode → owner → how it is measured
Failure mode	Mechanism	Measured by
F1 Injected or hidden input	Grounding check, hidden-text flag, SELF_CLAIMED cap	Adversarial injection set
F2 Unsupported claims	Evidence states and specificity	E1

can you explain the whole process in easy to understand manner

Think of EGCA as a hiring office with a strict rule: the person who reads the resume is never the person who decides anything.

The core idea

A resume is a list of things someone says about themselves. Most screening tools treat those statements as facts and score them. EGCA treats them as claims, then checks how much support each claim has, applies the company's mandatory rules separately, and only then ranks people.

Five jobs, each with its own owner:

Job	Who does it
Read the resume	The AI (reading only)
Check what's backed by evidence	Evidence checker
Check mandatory requirements	A separate rule engine
Put people in order	Scorer
Decide what a human should look at	Router

The AI never decides who qualifies. It only reads.

The walkthrough

Step 1: Intake. The resume is saved untouched, so there's always an original to point back to. The system also flags tricks like invisible white text.

Step 2: The AI reads it and writes down claims. For example: "Node.js, 3 years, backend work, from the experience section." Each claim must point to the exact spot in the resume. If the quoted text isn't really there, the claim is thrown out. This also limits prompt injection, where someone hides instructions in the resume, because whatever the AI writes is capped at "the candidate says so."

Step 3: Quick first ranking. Every resume gets a cheap, fast similarity score against the job description. This only answers "who looks relevant?" and it decides nothing.

Step 4: The gate. Careful checking is expensive, so we don't do it for everyone. The gate picks the top group to check properly. It picks more than we need, because some will fail the mandatory rules and we want to still have enough left afterward. The extra amount is calculated from how often people fail those rules, not guessed.

Step 5: The careful check. Each forwarded candidate goes through this in order, cheapest step first:

Evidence. Every claim is labeled by how much support exists: nothing known, just says so, inferred from related skills, backed by a real project, or formally assessed. "No evidence found" means we don't know, never they don't have it.
Mandatory rules (degree required, minimum years, and so on). A separate program checks these, and no high score can buy its way past them. It has three answers: pass, fail, or "can't tell yet." Fail stops everything for that candidate. "Can't tell" goes to a human.
Skill relationships. Fastify experience earns partial credit toward Express, clearly labeled "inferred," and it can never satisfy a mandatory requirement.
Score. Candidates who passed get a score from how well they fit and how strong their evidence is. Uncertainty is not subtracted here, so having no public profile doesn't quietly lower anyone's score.
Route. If the system is too unsure, or the resume looked tampered with, or the AI failed, the case goes to a human instead of getting an automatic answer.

Step 6: Expand if needed. Suppose you need 10 finalists and only 7 cleared. The system never loosens the rules. It pulls the next batch of candidates and repeats. It stops when it has enough cleared candidates and the top 10 stopped changing between batches. If it runs out of applicants, it says so and reports which requirement knocked out the most people. That can reveal that the job posting itself is unrealistic. Cases waiting on a human never count toward the 10.

Step 7: The paper trail. Every step is logged, so any rejection can be explained, and a checker replays the log on every run to confirm the rules were never broken.

What it does not do

It doesn't prove the people it picks are actually good engineers, doesn't prove hiring is fair, and only covers the resume stage, not interviews. Your tests use made-up candidates, so results are about how the system behaves, not about real hiring outcomes.

One open decision

If a resume just says "I have a degree" with no proof, should that count as a pass at this early screening stage? My suggestion is yes, since checking proof is a later hiring step, while a missing mention means "can't tell, ask a human." The rest of the design depends on your answer.
